The case for classics

Why Read Books That Are a Hundred Years Old?

Because they survived. Thousands of novels are published every year; almost all are forgotten within a decade. The books on these shelves did the opposite — they were read, reread, argued over, and loved by generation after generation. That survival is the strictest quality filter in existence.

Classics repay the effort they ask. Austen sharpens your eye for human vanity — including your own. Manto shows you what Partition did to ordinary souls in fewer pages than a newspaper feature. Mary Shelley's Frankenstein — written by an eighteen-year-old — asks questions about creation and responsibility that AI researchers are still answering.

And the language itself is a reward. Reading Ghalib in the original Urdu, or Doyle's crisp Victorian prose, trains your ear in a way no summary or adaptation can. These books are not homework; they are the original blockbusters — page-turners that happened to become immortal.
